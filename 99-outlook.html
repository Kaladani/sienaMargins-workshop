6  What is settled, and what is current work

How much weight each quantity can carry

A short guide to how much weight each quantity can carry.

6.1 Ready to use

The per-opportunity quantities are the ones to report: first differences on the tie-presence scale, second differences for interactions, each split by creation and maintenance margin, and averaged either at the observed waves or along model-implied ministeps.

They are well defined, they are implemented, and their interpretation is the one stated in Chapter 2: a probability change per decision opportunity, holding the decision situation fixed.

Ego-level covariates are perturbed across the whole choice set rather than one alternative at a time, for the reason given in the aside in Chapter 2. That happens by itself, so the per-tie numbers you get for a sender effect are sound; they are just a narrow summary of it. The probability-mass contrast is the fuller one, and it is what to reach for before drawing conclusions about covariate sender effects.

6.2 Comparability

Meta-analysing marginal effects rather than parameters is usually motivated by some combination of five claims. They hold up differently, and two are worth spelling out.

claim how it holds up
more meaningful than meta-analysing parameters yes — a pooled coefficient averages quantities whose scales need not match
challenges “the same model for all networks” yes, as a question worth asking rather than an answer already given
the exact same specification is less necessary in part, and it rests on an assumption — below
the main advantage is a common scale yes, though a common unit is not invariance — below
compare within networks, then those comparisons across networks yes, and arguably the strongest of the five

On the specification. Pooling probability-scale quantities is more forgiving than pooling coefficients, since the numbers at least share units. But an effect missing from one network’s specification does not simply drop out: what a model leaves unexplained is folded into everything else, so the remaining coefficients contract and their AMEs move with them. Treating a non-estimable effect as a true zero is an assumption about that network rather than something the scale provides — reasonable where the effect really is negligible there, and worth stating rather than leaving implicit.

On the common scale. The probability scale gives a common unit and an interpretable quantity: the effective impact of a mechanism on decisions, in that network’s actual conditions. What it does not give is invariance. Where two networks genuinely differ in how predictable their actors’ decisions are, the same underlying preference translates into different probability shifts — and an effect size that comes out differently there is reporting something true. A difference in AMEs identifies a difference in effective impact, noise included by construction; it does not identify a difference in preferences.

6.3 Accumulating to a whole period

Everything in Chapter 2 is per decision opportunity. A natural next question is what a mechanism contributes over a whole period between two waves, and the straightforward answer is to add the per-ministep effects along a simulated sequence.

This has one clear advantage: it absorbs the rate parameter. Two networks whose actors decide at very different frequencies can have the same per-opportunity effect but very different period-level pressure, and only the accumulated version reflects that — which makes it the natural candidate when opportunity rates differ across the networks being compared.

The scale needs care, though. An additive sum of probability changes is a count — extra ties created plus dissolutions avoided over the period — not a probability. It is not bounded, and it should not be set against a between-wave difference in tie proportions: a dyad that creates a tie and later drops it contributes twice to the count while changing prevalence not at all. So read ratios across specifications, which sit on a common scale, rather than levels on their own.

Getting from that count to a genuinely bounded, prevalence-scale period quantity is current work. The construction follows the tie’s own two-state history rather than summing increments; it is derived but not implemented, so it is not available in the software yet.

6.4 The rate parameter

Rate effects sit outside everything above, and that is a scope choice rather than an oversight: any count-level quantity mixes how often actors decide with what they choose given the chance, and separating the two needs its own treatment. The rate parameter is best read as governing how quickly a dyad’s situation can change, not where it ends up.

6.5 Further reading

The SAOM itself is set out in Snijders (2001) and, at book length, in Snijders and Steglich (2026). For marginal effects generally, Long and Mustillo (2021) is the closest thing to a standard reference, and Glasgow (2022) covers the discrete-choice side. On the ERGM side, Duxbury (2023) sets out the scaling problem and Duxbury (2026) develops effect sizes for structural terms; Indlekofer and Brandes (2013) is the nearest existing SAOM tool.
